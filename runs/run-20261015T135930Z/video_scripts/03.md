## Scene 2
Visual: Simple graphic with four icons appearing one at a time: scope turret, backpack, sleeping bag, binocular.
On-screen text: Four checks: Clicks. Loaded pack. Bag in your pack. Optic as a system.
Narration: Our take is that the reviewers' own caveats point to four checks. Verify your scope's click values. Load-test your pack at full weight. Check a sleeping bag's bulk and temperature margin in the pack you'll carry. And test any app-dependent optic as a system. These are our inferences, not tested protocols from the sources. Most of the reviews were done under lighter, shorter, or milder conditions than a multi-day foot carry.

## Scene 3
Visual: Close-up of a hand turning a scope turret, then an animated diagram of a ruler box with a second person standing beside it.
On-screen text: Source: AccurateShooter, Oct 15 | "36 indicated MOA = 144 clicks"
Narration: First, click values. An article on AccurateShooter's bulletin, published today, says you should test your scope to find out how much movement it actually delivers with each click. Its reader describes a ruler box test. It needs an assistant, who cranks on thirty-six indicated minutes of angle. That's one hundred forty-four clicks. The article also quotes Bryan Litz, who says more than fifty percent of the time, error in trajectory prediction at long range is actually scope adjustment. That's his claim, but it shows why this test is worth an afternoon.
Claims: [03-c1]

## Scene 4
Visual: Side-by-side graphic of a daypack bag labeled 3500 and a larger bag labeled 6000, with a short loop route sketched beside them.
On-screen text: Source: Rokslide, Icon 6 review | Tested: 3500 daypack | Loop: 1.2 miles, 510 vertical feet
Narration: Second, packs. Rokslide's reviewer tested the KUIU Icon 6 with the thirty-five hundred cubic inch daypack bag. The same review lists bigger multi-day bags, including a six thousand cubic inch freighter. His loops were one point two miles, with five hundred ten vertical feet gained and lost. He carried twenty-eight pounds of day gear, plus an added sixty-four pound simulated load. Put together, this suggests the heavy-load results come from a short loop on a day-sized bag.
Claims: [03-c2]

## Scene 5
Visual: Footage of a person rucking with a plate-carrying pack, then a graphic showing a hip belt and chest strap highlighted as optional add-ons.
On-screen text: Source: Ruck Authority, Rucker 5.0 at 1:03 and 3:00; GR1 at 2:05 | Tested at 20 lb | Free product, affiliate links
Narration: Ruck Authority's reviewer disclosed free product and affiliate links. He tested the GORUCK Rucker 5.0 with a twenty-pound plate and without the optional hip belt. He reported slightly sore traps, and he thought a hip belt might ease that. He also tested the GR1 at twenty pounds, without the chest strap and hip belt that GORUCK sells separately. He said he didn't miss the chest strap. He added that experienced ruckers say a sternum strap matters when the weight goes up. Our take is to test your pack at full loaded weight, with the belt and strap you would actually use.
Claims: [03-c3]

## Scene 6
Visual: A sleeping bag in a stuff sack being squeezed into a mid-sized pack, then a thermometer graphic near freezing.
On-screen text: Source: Treeline Review, Magma 30 | Source: Vortex podcast with Argali, at 27:01 | Manufacturer view, Mammoth sponsor host
Narration: Third, the sleeping bag. Treeline Review's reviewer said the REI Magma 30 took up a significant amount of space in her forty-four liter pack. She would not push it much further below freezing. On her coldest tested night she didn't feel cold, but she was wearing a wool baselayer and a long sleeve top. Treeline works on an affiliate model. Separately, Argali's Brad Brooks said on Vortex's podcast that many companies use limit or extreme ratings. He says he uses comfort ratings, which still won't suit every sleeper. That's a manufacturer's view, on a podcast hosted by a Mammoth sponsor. Still, it gives you a question to ask: is this rating comfort, or limit?

## Scene 7
Visual: Close-up of a rangefinding binocular, a phone showing an app, and a small battery, arranged on a table.
On-screen text: Source: Rokslide, Tract Toric LRF review | 2 lb 5.5 oz | One CR2 battery | Phone app required for wind data
Narration: Fourth, any device that depends on an app. Rokslide's reviewer reported that the Tract Toric LRF weighs two pounds, five and a half ounces, and runs on a single C R two battery. He said the phone app is a necessary go-between to combine its ranges with wind data from a connected wind meter. He also saw the diopter shift once. Our take is to test any such device as a system, with your wind meter, your phone, and the battery use that comes with it.
