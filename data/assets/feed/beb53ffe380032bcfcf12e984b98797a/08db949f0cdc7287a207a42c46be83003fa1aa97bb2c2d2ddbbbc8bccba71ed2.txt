The short answer

To start rucking for weight loss, carry 5-10 lbs for 20-30 minutes on level ground, two or three times a week, at a pace where you can still talk in full sentences. Hold that load for the first month and add time and sessions before weight. The first goal is to make loaded walking repeatable.

The simple beginner prescription:

Rucking works for fat loss because it increases the cost of walking without turning every session into a hard run. Added load raises metabolic demand, but the movement pattern stays familiar, repeatable, and low skill. For most beginners, that combination beats a harder workout they only do for three weeks.

How many calories does rucking burn?

The science behind rucking calories comes from military research. The Pandolf equation, published by U.S. Army researchers in 1977, is the long-standing model for predicting energy expenditure while carrying a load. Unlike generic calorie calculators that assume level terrain and no load, it accounts for five variables:

Your body weight: heavier people burn more calories.

The weight you're carrying: the load itself takes energy to move.

Your walking speed: faster means more calories per minute, and the cost rises faster than the speed does.

Terrain grade: every percent of uphill grade adds a large, steady increase in cost.

Surface type: soft or uneven surfaces such as sand, mud and rough trail cost more than pavement.

A 180-pound person carrying 30 pounds at 3.5 miles per hour on flat pavement burns about 410 calories an hour, against about 365 walking the same route without a pack. That 13 percent is less than a lot of rucking content promises. The bigger lever is the route: the same ruck up a 5 percent grade is about 635 calories an hour. Army researchers have found that older equations, Pandolf included, underestimate the cost of heavy load carriage (Looney et al., 2022), so treat these as conservative.

The Ruck Authority Progressive Load Protocol

Weight-loss rucking plans usually go wrong in one of two ways: they start too heavy, or they chase intensity before the habit exists. The Ruck Authority Progressive Load Protocol avoids both by sequencing the stress in the order your body can adapt to it:

Load first: start with a light, stable pack.

Frequency second: build from 2 rucks per week to 3-4.

Distance third: add weekly miles once your joints feel normal.

Intensity last: add hills, pace, or heavier weight only after the base is built.

For beginners, the protocol is intentionally conservative:

Treat every load in the protocol as a ceiling to stay under, and use the lighter end if you're unsure. A 120-pound beginner doesn't need the same load as a 220-pound beginner, and an already active person may tolerate more volume than someone coming off the couch. If a step makes your stride shorter or your recovery slower, repeat the previous week.

The citable rule: For weight loss, start with 5-10 lbs, build to 3-4 rucks per week, and reach 8-12 weekly miles before pushing past 20 lbs. Consistency creates the calorie burn. Heavy load is optional.

It accounts for body mass, load mass, walking speed, terrain grade, and a terrain coefficient. Newer equations from the same Army research institute fit heavy loads more closely (Looney et al., 2022). Our calculator uses the original 1977 equation, on flat and uphill ground.

These are estimates based on average metabolism and efficiency. Some people burn a little more and some a little less, but the equation is close enough for practical planning, and it ranks the options correctly: more load costs a bit more, and more grade costs a lot more.

Why rucking works for fat loss (when other things haven't)

It's sustainable

Running injury rates vary widely with the population and the definition of injury: a systematic review of long-distance runners found yearly lower-limb injury rates anywhere from 19 to 79 percent (van Gent et al., 2007). Those studies don't establish that recreational rucking has a lower annual injury rate or better adherence. Choose the activity you enjoy and can recover from.

Rucking feels like walking because it is walking, just with weight. You don't have to become "a runner" first, and the first ruck is well within reach of anyone who already walks for half an hour. That matters for fat loss because a plan only works while you keep doing it. A moderate program you repeat for three months will do more than an ambitious one you drop after three weeks.

It keeps the effort moderate

Rucking at a conversational pace usually lands in Zone 2, commonly defined as roughly 60-70% of maximum heart rate. At that intensity fat supplies a larger share of the fuel than it does during hard efforts. Over weeks, though, the total calorie deficit decides fat loss far more than the fuel mix during any one session. The practical advantage of a moderate effort is that you can do more of it, more often, and recover between sessions.

For many people, a ruck at 3.5 mph with a light-to-moderate pack sits in that range. A GPS watch with heart rate monitoring like the Garmin Instinct 3 shows whether it does for you. Running tends to push beginners into higher zones, which feels harder and needs more recovery.

For the complete breakdown of Zone 2 physiology and how to structure your rucking for maximum fat oxidation, see our Zone 2 rucking guide.

It adds a light strength stimulus

Every ruck is a loaded carry. The pack makes your posterior chain, trunk and legs work harder than they do on an ordinary walk, which is a useful stimulus for someone starting from little or no training.

It isn't a substitute for lifting, and that matters in a calorie deficit, when some of the weight you lose can come from muscle. In a meta-analysis of exercise trials, resistance training raised resting metabolic rate by about 96 calories a day while aerobic training did not change it significantly (MacKenzie-Shalders et al., 2020). Pair rucking with one or two short strength sessions a week to hold on to muscle while the fat comes off.

The load itself moves the scale

The best controlled evidence that carrying weight changes body composition comes from weighted-vest trials, and a 2026 meta-analysis pooled them.

A 2026 meta-analysis in Osteoporosis International (Wang et al.) combined nine randomized trials covering 368 middle-aged and older adults. People who exercised wearing a weighted vest lost about 2.7 kg more body fat than those who kept their usual activity. More useful for anyone already active: adding the vest to an existing exercise program removed a further 0.7 kg of fat compared with the same exercise without it. The authors rated the fat-mass evidence high certainty. These were vests rather than packs and the participants were mostly older adults, so read the numbers as the direction of the effect rather than a forecast for your own ruck.

Lower impact, but still a load to build

Peak ground forces in walking sit around 1.2 times body weight and climb to about 2.5 times in fast running (Keller et al., 1996). A pack raises the walking forces roughly in proportion to the load (Birrell et al., 2007), so a moderate ruck still lands well below a run. Lower impact still means some impact. Some conditioned people tolerate frequent loaded walks, but beginners shouldn't copy that frequency automatically: pack weight, duration, terrain and other training all contribute to the demand. Reduce the schedule if soreness, pain or fatigue keeps accumulating.

More sessions per week means more total calorie burn. Four one-hour rucks a week at roughly 400 to 600 calories each (flat to hilly) add up to 1,600 to 2,400 calories of activity. About 85 calories of each hour is what a 180-pound person would burn sitting still anyway, so the net extra is roughly 1,250 to 2,050. Paired with a modest food deficit, that adds up.

The rucking weight loss program

The protocol above gets you through the first eight weeks. The following 12-week framework is the broader program for turning that start into a real fat-loss block. Each phase builds on the previous one, gradually increasing the stress on your system to drive adaptation.

Phase 1: Foundation (Weeks 1-4)

The first month is about building the habit and letting your body adapt to loaded walking. Your tendons, ligaments, and stabilizer muscles haven't done this before. Your feet need to toughen up. You need to find good routes and integrate rucking into your schedule.

Keep the load light. Five to ten pounds feels like something at first without being heavy enough to cause problems while your form settles. A flat cast-iron ruck plate like the Yes4All sits against your back and won't shift mid-stride the way dumbbells or loose water bottles will. The pace should be conversational: you should be able to talk, maybe not sing, during the ruck.

Two or three sessions a week creates a stimulus without overloading tissues that haven't carried weight before, and it fits most schedules without rearranging them.

Not sure exactly how much weight to start with? Our ruck weight calculator gives you a planning ceiling from your bodyweight and fitness level in about 30 seconds. For everything you need to gear up, see the complete rucking gear guide.

Phase 2: Build (Weeks 5-8)

If the first month felt repeatable, with no lingering soreness in your feet, knees or back, you're ready to do more. Add frequency and distance before load.

Moving to four sessions a week is where the weekly calorie burn starts to climb: roughly 700-1,500 calories of rucking a week, on top of your normal daily expenditure. Add distance gradually; about half a mile per week is a manageable step.

Add load in small steps too, and only after a week at the current weight felt easy. There's no automatic weekly jump: repeat or reduce a week when needed, because a calendar isn't evidence that you're ready. The old 10% rule is a reasonable brake, though the evidence behind any exact percentage is thin. Choose footwear suited to the terrain, keep the route manageable and change one training variable at a time. Make one of the weekly sessions your longer ruck (3-4 miles), long enough to build aerobic capacity without making recovery a problem.

Phase 3: Burn (Weeks 9-12)

By the third phase, loaded walking should feel routine, and this is where the weekly calorie output peaks. Four or five sessions a week at roughly 300-550 calories each, more on the hill day, comes to about 1,350-2,900 calories a week from rucking, before counting your baseline metabolism or any deficit from food.

Hills carry the extra intensity rather than speed. On the Pandolf numbers, a 5 percent grade raises the cost of the same walk far more than an extra 10 pounds does, without requiring a faster walking pace. Hills add their own challenge, particularly on descents, and they aren't proven to be a lower-injury-risk option, so keep hill sessions short at first and adjust the rest of the week. One session a week includes hills; the others are flat or mixed terrain at a moderate pace.

Nutrition guidelines for rucking weight loss

Rucking creates the opportunity for fat loss through caloric expenditure. Nutrition determines whether you actually realize that opportunity or accidentally eat back all the calories you burn.

The cardinal rule: Don't eat back all your rucking calories. A common mistake is "I just burned 500 calories, so I can eat an extra 500 calories today." At best, this means your rucking effort created zero deficit. At worst, you slightly overcompensate and gain weight while exercising consistently.

Instead, let the rucking add to your overall deficit while your intake stays where it was.

Protein matters more in a deficit. The International Society of Sports Nutrition puts most exercising adults at 1.4-2.0 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight a day, roughly 0.6-0.9 grams per pound (Jäger et al., 2017). If you're carrying a lot of extra weight, base the number on your goal weight. Eating in a deficit raises the chance that some of the weight you lose is muscle; enough protein, plus the strength work below, helps keep it.

Don't create an excessive deficit. A deficit of about 500 calories a day works out to roughly a pound a week on paper and is manageable for most people. A 1,000-calorie daily deficit is aggressive and can backfire: energy and recovery suffer, and tired legs under a loaded pack are more likely to pick up an overuse injury.

Drink to the conditions. The American College of Sports Medicine's starting point for longer exercise is about 0.4-0.8 liters an hour (roughly 13-27 fluid ounces), more for bigger people in the heat and less in cool weather, adjusted to your own sweat rate (Sawka et al., 2007). Carrying weight raises your metabolic heat, so warm-day rucks need more. For longer or hotter sessions, an electrolyte mix replaces the sodium you sweat out. A pack with good weight distribution helps you carry the water comfortably; see our best rucking backpacks guide for packs designed to handle full loads.

Timing around rucks: A light carbohydrate snack 30-60 minutes before a ruck (a banana, a slice of toast with almond butter) gives you energy without upsetting your stomach. Afterwards, a meal with protein and carbohydrate supports recovery; the exact timing matters less than getting it that day.

We are not registered dietitians. These are general guidelines based on sports nutrition research. If you have significant metabolic concerns or are managing a medical condition, consult a professional for personalized nutrition planning.

Can rucking be your only exercise?

For fat loss specifically, yes, as long as your eating keeps you in a calorie deficit. Fat loss isn't the same as all-round fitness, though. We dig into this question in detail in our dedicated article: Can rucking be your only exercise?

The short answer: add 1-2 days per week of strength training (even light bodyweight work) to preserve muscle mass and improve body composition beyond just scale weight.

Tracking your progress

The scale is a poor short-term feedback mechanism for body composition change. A 1-2 pound daily fluctuation based on hydration, sodium intake, and the menstrual cycle is completely normal and tells you nothing about fat loss.

Instead, track weekly averages: weigh yourself daily and average the week, then compare week-to-week. A 500-calorie daily deficit works out to roughly a pound a week on paper; the first weeks often show more as water shifts, and the rate usually slows later. For a realistic picture of what changes at 30, 60, and 90 days, see our rucking before and after timeline.

Also track:

Circumference measurements (waist, hips, chest), which often change before the scale does

Progress photos (every 4 weeks), because visual change is sometimes more motivating than scale weight

Rucking metrics: load, distance, pace, heart rate and RPE. Look for a faster pace at the same weight, or more weight at the same pace.

A GPS watch with built-in heart rate makes the rucking-metric piece automatic. If you want a do-everything lifestyle tracker rather than a rugged outdoor watch, our sister site Kit Authority's Garmin Vivoactive 5 review covers that everyday-fitness option.

Early on (weeks 1-4), the scale may not move as much as you'd hope. New training often brings some water retention as muscles adapt, which can hide fat loss for a few weeks. Waist measurements and the weekly average are better guides than any single weigh-in, and the trend tends to be clearer by week 8.

Common weight loss mistakes with rucking

A 30-pound load in week 2 sounds ambitious, and it's the classic way to pick up an overuse injury that costs you more weeks than a lighter start ever would. Hold one light load for the first month, then add weight in small steps only after a week that felt easy.

Burning 500 calories on a ruck doesn't buy an extra 500 calories of food. Count the session toward your weekly deficit and leave your intake where it was.

Rucking burns calories. Strength training preserves muscle mass in a deficit, which is critical for body composition. They work together. Add 1-2 days of bodyweight or gym work to the program.

Rest days are when adaptation happens. Rucking 6-7 days per week in a caloric deficit piles up fatigue, which makes the deficit harder to hold. Don't count on extra sessions to speed up your metabolism either: in the MacKenzie-Shalders et al. (2020) meta-analysis in the Journal of Sports Sciences, aerobic training didn't significantly raise resting metabolic rate, while resistance training raised it by about 96 calories a day. Four or five rucks a week plus two short strength sessions is a sustainable mix.

Weight loss is nonlinear. Some weeks drop a few pounds, some stall, and some tick up before falling again as water and food volume change. That's normal. Look at the 4-week trend rather than the daily number.

A useful plan combines repeatable activity, nutrition that suits the goal, enough sleep and room to recover. There isn't a proven best rucking frequency for fat loss. Start with a schedule you can sustain and adjust from your own trend.

For fat loss specifically, three items matter most: a pack with a plate pocket that keeps load against your upper back (not your lumbar), a plate heavy enough to drive intensity without forcing pace, and cushioned shoes that keep your joints quiet over daily sessions. That's it.
