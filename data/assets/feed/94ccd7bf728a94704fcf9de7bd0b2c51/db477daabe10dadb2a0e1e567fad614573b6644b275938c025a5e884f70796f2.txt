Longevity medicine doctors like Peter Attia have a problem with how most people exercise: they aren't training the capacities that best predict how long, and how well, people live.

In a 60 Minutes segment that aired in October 2025, Attia put it this way: "When you look at things like cardiorespiratory fitness, when you look at muscle mass, when you look at strength, they have a much higher association than things like even cholesterol and blood pressure." In the same segment he took the CBS crew on a ruck, a hike with weighted backpacks.

Rucking appeals to longevity-minded people because one session works on several of those capacities at once. Attia and author Michael Easter discuss the benefits of rucking in a clip from their conversation on Attia's podcast. Here's what the science supports, and where it runs out.

The longevity metrics that matter most

Longevity medicine focuses on a handful of physical capacities that track with both lifespan and healthspan:

VO2 max gets the most attention. Attia and his team describe it as the key indicator of overall health and longevity: your body's maximum oxygen uptake during hard exercise. In the 60 Minutes segment he argued that fitness tests like this are more predictive of how long you'll live than standard bloodwork.

Zone 2 aerobic fitness is your capacity for sustained, moderate-intensity work, the kind you can keep up while still talking. It reflects how well your muscles and mitochondria use fuel, a capacity that tends to decline with age and inactivity.

Bone mineral density shapes fracture risk. As covered in our research on rucking for bone density, bone mass peaks in early adulthood and declines from there, faster in women after menopause, which makes loaded exercise increasingly important with age.

Muscular strength and power predict functional capacity in later years. The ability to carry, lift, and move under load translates directly to independence and fall prevention.

Most training targets one or two of these well. Running builds aerobic capacity and is itself a high-impact bone load, but it is hard on joints for many older adults. Lifting builds strength but does little for aerobic fitness. Rucking touches aerobic fitness, leg strength and bone density in one session, at a lower impact than running, though it does not replace dedicated hard intervals or heavy strength training.

Carrying weight at a steady aerobic pace puts mechanical load through bones and joints while keeping the heart in an aerobic zone. Cardio and strength work usually deliver those separately; a ruck delivers a moderate dose of both.

Why rucking hits the longevity sweet spot

Here's how a loaded walk maps onto those capacities.

Zone 2 cardio: For many people, walking with a loaded pack at a conversational pace, especially with some hills, lands in an easy aerobic range that roughly matches what longevity doctors call Zone 2. Where you land depends on your fitness, the load and the terrain, so check it with a heart-rate monitor or the talk test rather than assuming. Attia recommends Zone 2 as the bulk of a well-designed cardio program.

As detailed in our Zone 2 rucking guide, training at this intensity builds aerobic base and helps your muscles use fat for fuel. The advantage over cycling or swimming at the same effort is that rucking also loads your skeleton.

Bone loading: Weight carried on your back increases the force through your spine, hips, and legs on every step. Bone responds best to loads that are higher than usual and applied with some speed, which is why jumping and heavy lifting outperform plain walking in trials. A ruck sits in between: more force per step than ordinary walking, far less impact than running. Weighted-vest trials suggest that loaded exercise protects bone better than inactivity, but a 2025 trial found a vest did not prevent hip bone loss during dieting, so treat rucking as one bone-loading tool rather than the whole answer.

Aerobic demand and VO2 max: Load carriage raises the oxygen cost of walking at a given pace, and hills raise it far more. On flat ground the effect of load is modest: by the Pandolf load-carriage equation, a 180-pound person walking at 3.5 mph burns about 365 calories an hour unloaded and about 410 with a 30-pound pack, while a 5% grade pushes the loaded figure to about 635. Steep, loaded hikes can push you into hard efforts that challenge VO2 max, and you can adjust intensity with weight, pace, or terrain instead of running interval protocols. For dedicated VO2 max work, Attia treats hard intervals as a separate pillar of training.

Start with 5 to 10 pounds for 20 to 30 minutes on level ground and hold that load for the first month. Aim for a pace where you can hold a conversation and feel slightly breathless going uphill. A heart-rate watch like the COROS PACE 4 is one of the simplest ways to confirm you're staying in an easy aerobic range rather than drifting into a harder effort. If you want to pick one up, the PACE 4 is the budget-friendly watch we steer most ruckers toward.

The efficiency factor that longevity doctors like

What makes rucking appealing to busy people is that one session works on several systems at once.

A Fortune profile of his exercise routine describes Attia carrying a weight plate in a backpack and walking up and down hills around his neighborhood. A session like that provides:

Cardiovascular conditioning through sustained elevated heart rate

Lower body strength development from load carriage

Core stabilization from maintaining posture under load

Bone loading from the added weight

Balance and proprioceptive training on uneven terrain

That overlap is the efficiency argument. Rucking won't replace every session in a longevity program (Attia's own framework, laid out on his podcast, trains Zone 2, VO2 max, strength and stability as separate pillars), but it lets one outing cover easy aerobic volume and some loading, which frees time for the rest.

Three or four rucks a week, mixed with strength training, is a sustainable, lower-impact base that many people find easier to recover from than a schedule built around high-intensity sessions.

The practical longevity advantage

Beyond the physiology, rucking has practical advantages that matter for sticking with exercise over decades.

Low skill barrier: Walking with weight has almost no learning curve. You can start immediately without scheduling around classes or facilities.

Minimal equipment needs: You can start with a well-fitting pack you already own and a stable load. If you later want dedicated hardware, a pack such as the GORUCK Rucker 5.0 pairs elevated plate pockets with GORUCK's lifetime guarantee. That is an equipment choice, not a requirement for the training stimulus.

Scalable intensity: As fitness improves or declines with age, you can adjust weight, distance, or terrain to keep the stimulus appropriate without switching to an entirely different activity.

Social compatibility: The conversational pace makes rucking easy to do with other people, and social connection is something longevity-minded doctors consistently emphasize. Attia's own 60 Minutes ruck was framed partly as time outdoors with company.

Weather independence: Outdoor rucking is ideal, but you can keep training on a treadmill or stair climber when conditions don't cooperate.

Sustainability is the whole game for longevity benefits. A routine you keep for decades will do more than an ambitious one you abandon in a few months.

What the research shows about weighted walking and aging

Long-term longevity studies on rucking specifically don't exist yet; the activity has only become a mainstream fitness conversation in the last few years. The closest evidence comes from load-carriage research and weighted walking trials with vests. The most useful recent example is a 2026 meta-analysis of nine weighted-vest trials (Wang et al., Osteoporosis International), which found that vest-plus-exercise programs improved hip and spine bone density and reduced body fat in middle-aged and older adults compared with usual activity. It also found no confirmed extra bone benefit from adding a vest for people already exercising. Our rucking research 2026 roundup covers it and the other studies published this year.

The military's use of load carriage as conditioning is another data point. Loaded marching has been part of soldier preparation for generations because it builds aerobic and musculoskeletal capacity together at a lower joint-impact cost than running, which is the same trade-off longevity-focused exercise prioritizes.

For women, Zone 2 rucking offers a moderate, sustainable intensity that pairs well with strength training and adds bone loading to aerobic work.

Starting a longevity-focused rucking protocol

The progression below is our synthesis of principles longevity doctors emphasize (gradual loading, mostly Zone 2, consistency over intensity) applied to rucking. It's not a protocol prescribed by any single physician; treat it as a sensible default to adjust to your own training history and recovery.

Weeks 1 to 4: Foundation phase

Weight: 5 to 10 lb, held steady for the month

Duration: 20 to 30 minutes

Frequency: 3x per week

Intensity: Conversational pace (able to speak in full sentences)

Weeks 5 to 12: Development phase

Weight: add small amounts as sessions feel easy, working toward roughly 10 to 15% of body weight

Duration: 30 to 45 minutes

Frequency: 3 to 4x per week

Intensity: Mostly Zone 2, with some harder hill sessions

Week 13 and beyond: Maintenance phase

Weight: around 15 to 20% of body weight is common for experienced ruckers; heavier is optional

Duration: 45 to 90 minutes

Frequency: 3 to 4x per week

Intensity: Primarily Zone 2 with periodic challenging sessions

The principle is gradual progression that allows adaptation without excessive stress or injury risk. The goal is improvement you can sustain over decades, not quick gains you can't maintain.

For equipment, focus on a properly fitted pack that carries the weight high and close to your back rather than pulling on your shoulders. Our rucking backpacks roundup covers picks for every budget, from entry-level day packs to purpose-built rucksacks designed for sustained loaded walking.

Track your rucking sessions by time and perceived exertion rather than obsessing over pace or distance. The longevity benefits come from consistency and appropriate intensity, not performance metrics.

The longevity case for rucking

Attia's case for rucking rests on the idea that fitness predicts healthy aging better than most lab values, and that a loaded walk trains several kinds of fitness at once. That logic holds up. Rucking combines aerobic work, leg strength and bone loading in a single, sustainable activity that people of most ages can start.

Author Michael Easter, who discussed rucking with Attia, argues that carrying loads over distance is something the human body evolved to do well, which helps explain why it suits so many people.

The limit is that no long-term trial has tested rucking for longevity outcomes, and it doesn't replace dedicated strength or interval training. As a base that most people can keep up for years, the case for adding loaded walking to a longevity routine is strong.

If a backpack feels like a bigger step than you're ready for, the gentler on-ramp is a weighted vest on your normal walk. Our sister site Kit Authority's weighted vest walking for beginners guide starts there.
