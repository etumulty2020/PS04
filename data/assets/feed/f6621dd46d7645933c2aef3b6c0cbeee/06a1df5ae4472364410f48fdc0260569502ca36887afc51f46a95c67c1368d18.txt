Plenty of 2026 fitness trends come with an app, a wearable or a subscription. One of the more talked-about ones needs a backpack: walking with a weighted pack.

Rucking, carrying a loaded pack while walking, keeps turning up in trend coverage. FitNKC calls it "The Backpack Workout Taking Over Fitness in 2026" and describes it as one of the year's most popular fitness trends. Lifemaxx's 2026 trends roundup doesn't name rucking, but it lists functional fitness, the lifting, carrying and moving category rucking belongs to, among the trends that still dominate gym programming.

Below is what's behind the attention, and how to start without overdoing it.

Why Rucking Is Having Its Moment

The Back-to-Basics Movement

Josh Snyder of FitNKC puts it this way: "People are going back to basics. Walking with weight. Training with purpose. Asking their bodies to do what they were designed to do: push, pull, carry, and move through real space with real resistance."

That fits a broader appetite for training that works without being complicated.

The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 trends survey put wearable technology first, fitness programs for older adults second and exercise for weight management third, with functional fitness training at number ten. Lifemaxx's summary of the report describes the focus shifting toward "quality, personalisation and long-term wellbeing".

High-Profile Advocates

GQ's 2024 rucking explainer credited a few well-known voices with spreading the habit:

Peter Attia, MD, the longevity physician, has praised rucking on his podcast, The Drive

Andrew Huberman was filmed rucking with bowhunter Cameron Hanes

Michael Easter's book The Comfort Crisis devotes a chapter to recasting rucking from a military requirement into recreational exercise

GORUCK founder Jason McCarthy told GQ the book was the turning point: "Influential people read that book and started adopting it," he said, naming Attia as someone who read it and started rucking a lot.

Measurable Business Growth

McCarthy also told GQ that GORUCK's sales rose 40 percent in 2023. That is the company's own figure rather than audited data, but weighted vests point the same way: Axios reported that sales jumped more than 50 percent to 27 million dollars in the 12 months ending May 2025, citing market research firm Circana. If you're weighing the vest side of that boom against a pack, our sister site Kit Authority's weighted vest vs rucking breakdown covers which suits a general-fitness routine.

For ruckers, it suggests the activity is reaching people well outside military and tactical circles.

What Rucking Trains

Functional Movement Benefits

Rucking trains your body to carry a load while moving, which is much of what people have always needed their bodies to do.

The movement pattern engages:

Posterior chain muscles (glutes, hamstrings, erector spinae)

Core stabilizers working to maintain posture under load

Cardiovascular system through sustained moderate-intensity effort

Rucking is lower impact than running at the same pace while still working your heart, lungs and legs. That makes it a reasonable option for many people who don't tolerate running, provided they build the load slowly. If you have a joint condition or an injury history, check with a clinician first.

Longevity and Anti-Aging Benefits

The timing of rucking's mainstream moment coincides with increased interest in longevity and anti-aging protocols. As FitNKC notes, "Researchers are confirming what consistent training actually does at the cellular level. They are measuring how movement changes blood proteins tied to aging."

Rucking touches several things linked to healthy aging:

Weight-bearing exercise, which supports bone health, although how much loaded walking alone changes bone density is still unsettled

Cardiovascular fitness from sustained zone 2 effort

Functional strength that carries over to daily activities

Time outdoors, which many people find good for their mood

How Rucking Fits the 2026 Fitness Landscape

Technology Integration Without Dependence

Wearables top the ACSM list, and rucking works with or without them: a watch can track pace and heart rate, but the activity itself needs only a pack and your feet.

Looking back at 2025, Lifemaxx observed: "The best gyms used technology to enhance coaching, not to replace the trainer. Data became the starting point for conversations about recovery, load and progress."

Accessibility and Inclusivity

Rucking costs little to start. Any sturdy backpack and household items for weight will do, which puts it within reach on most budgets and in most places.

Community Building

Rucking is also social. Organized events, ruck clubs and veteran groups give people a reason to show up together and keep showing up.

The rucking community has deep roots in military culture, with its emphasis on teamwork and mutual support, and many civilians are drawn to exactly that.

Getting Started: Your First Ruck

Equipment Essentials

The Basics:

A sturdy backpack that fits snugly (it doesn't need to be tactical, though a daily-carry pack like the 5.11 RUSH 12 has the padded straps a loaded walk needs)

Weight (start with books, water bottles, or dedicated ruck plates)

Comfortable, supportive walking shoes; a cushioned shoe like the Hoka Bondi 9 is a reasonable choice for pavement

Starting Weights:

Beginners: 5-10 pounds

Intermediate: 20-30 pounds

Advanced: 35+ pounds

Start conservatively with weight. It's better to ruck regularly with lighter loads than to go too heavy too fast and risk injury or burnout. You can always add weight as your body adapts.

Your First Month Protocol

Week 1-2:

Duration: 20-30 minutes on level ground

Weight: 5-10 pounds

Frequency: 2-3 times per week

Pace: Conversational (you should be able to talk while rucking)

Week 3-4:

Duration: 30-40 minutes

Weight: the same load as weeks 1-2

Frequency: 3 times per week

Make one session per week a little longer

Safety Considerations

Common Mistakes to Avoid:

Going too heavy too fast

Ignoring proper pack fit

Skipping warmup and cooldown

Training on concrete exclusively

Red Flags to Watch For:

Sharp pain in feet, knees, or back

Excessive fatigue that doesn't resolve with rest

Persistent soreness lasting more than 48 hours

No trial has tested a specific weekly percentage for adding ruck weight. Military load-carriage research (Knapik, 2014) points to lighter loads, better load distribution, appropriate physical training and suitable equipment as the main ways to reduce injuries. Our guidance: hold your starting load for the first month, then add weight in small steps only when sessions feel easy and nothing hurts the next day.

Why This Trend Has Staying Power

Several things work in rucking's favor over the long run:

Proven Track Record

Soldiers have carried loaded packs for as long as there have been armies, and ruck marching remains a staple of military training. The civilian fitness version is the newer part.

Scalable Difficulty

You can make rucking as easy or as challenging as needed by adjusting weight, distance, terrain, or pace. This scalability means people can progress continuously without hitting arbitrary ceilings.

Practical Application

The strength and endurance built through rucking directly translates to real-world activities: hiking with gear, carrying groceries, moving furniture, or simply maintaining functional capacity as you age.

Low Maintenance

Unlike complex fitness systems that require ongoing learning or equipment updates, rucking's simplicity makes it sustainable long-term. Once you understand the basics, you can ruck anywhere, anytime.

Why the trend may last

The fitness industry's interest in rucking reflects demand for training that is simple, functional, and social. Rucking offers all three without much setup.

Whether you're drawn to rucking by the longevity benefits, the simplicity, or the community aspect, the fundamentals remain the same: start light, progress gradually, and focus on consistency over intensity.

You can start with a backpack, a manageable load, and a walking route. That low barrier may help rucking remain useful after the current wave of attention passes.
