How GORUCK describes the technique

In the September 24 email, co-founder Jason McCarthy describes shuffling as a sustainable effort between walking and running. His example is 30 lb, compared with the 45 or 60 lb he carries on other days. He promotes cardiovascular demand, stamina and calorie expenditure, and pitches a specific swap: if you jog an 8 to 9 minute mile, put 30 lb on your back and shuffle instead. The email's list of benefits includes saving your knees. He also explicitly cautions against running with a heavy ruck.

His three setup cues are to use a manageable load, go without a hip belt, and hold the pack close and high. The email directs experienced ruckers toward a faster session and links the Basic Rucker to its Ruck or Die events.

The older white paper gives the movement more detail: short, quick steps, feet kept low, and one foot remaining on the ground. It describes this as an advanced technique to try with less weight after building experience. That matters: calling any loaded jog a shuffle doesn't faithfully describe GORUCK's instructions.

The email's 75% effort is a coaching cue. It doesn't specify a percentage of maximum heart rate, heart-rate reserve or a measured threshold. We shouldn't turn it into a precise Zone 2 target.

What the airborne shuffle studies found

US soldiers sometimes use the same short-stride gait on loaded marches and call it the airborne shuffle. Researchers at Middle Tennessee State University have measured it against loaded walking in a 2014 master's thesis and a 2017 doctoral dissertation. Both were reviewed by university committees rather than journal referees, and both used small samples.

Brenes (2014) tested 20 Army ROTC cadets, average age 22, carrying a standard combat load at 2.5, 3.0 and 3.5 mph. At every speed, shuffling raised oxygen cost, heart rate and respiratory exchange ratio compared with walking (p < .001). The thesis concludes that the shuffle is not a more economical way to carry a combat load than walking.

Clark (2017) studied 11 soldiers and defines the shuffle as a deliberately shortened stride. At 3.0 mph it produced shorter strides, shorter stance and swing phases and a quicker cadence than walking. Left to pick their own pace, soldiers shuffled faster than they walked, and oxygen use, heart rate, breathing and perceived effort all rose with it. Clark suggests the shuffle may reduce stress on the lower limbs, but the dissertation measured gait timing and energy cost, not joint forces. Its recommendation is to prioritize walking on loaded marches because the shuffle costs more energy and feels harder.

Read together, the two projects support half of GORUCK's pitch: the shuffle is harder work than walking at the same speed, which is the point if you want more cardiovascular demand from a lighter pack. The knee half appears only as a possibility in Clark's discussion. Both projects also compared the shuffle with walking at 2.5 to 3.5 mph, roughly a 17 to 24 minute mile. Neither tested GORUCK's actual swap of a 30 lb shuffle for an unloaded 8 to 9 minute-mile jog.

What load-carriage running research adds

For the comparison GORUCK makes, against jogging, the closest evidence comes from studies of walking and running with and without load. They help explain why technique and load matter. They can't settle which option is safer for your knees.

How you move changes the forces

Keller and colleagues' 1996 study measured ground reaction forces in 23 recreational athletes walking and running at different speeds. Its slow-jogging condition produced particularly high forces and loading rates, associated with more vertical movement.

That gives a biomechanical reason to pay attention to bounce. It doesn't validate GORUCK's loaded technique: participants weren't performing a standardized ruck shuffle, and the study didn't measure subsequent knee injuries. A form cue can be sensible without having its own clinical trial.

Adding weight changes the task

Birrell and colleagues measured increased ground reaction forces during military load carriage. Silder, Besier and Delp studied 27 people running at a fixed speed with progressively heavier weight vests. Added load increased peak vertical force and changed leg stiffness and posture.

Two further running studies looked closer at the joints. Brown and colleagues had 15 male military personnel run carrying about 6, 20 and 40 kg; heavier loads raised peak knee, hip and trunk flexion moments during steady running. Liew, Morris and Netto ran 31 people at 3 to 5 m/s, roughly 5:20 to 9:00 per mile, carrying up to 20% of body weight. More load increased joint power late in stance and altered ankle, knee and hip angles.

Those results don't prove that a loaded shuffle is worse than an unloaded jog. The pack, gait, speed and person all matter, and none of these experiments tested that comparison. They do give us a reason to treat added weight as a real training demand, even when the movement feels smooth.

Knee safety needs a different kind of evidence

Force measured under the foot isn't the same thing as force inside the knee, and neither is an injury rate. We can't divide two ground-force numbers and call the result a knee-safety advantage.

To establish the email's knee claim, we'd want a direct comparison with clearly defined loads, speeds and technique, followed by relevant joint measurements or injuries over time. None of the sources on this page supplies that, including the two airborne shuffle projects. The fair conclusion is that a knee-protection advantage over jogging isn't established here, not that shuffling has been shown to damage knees.

Army injury-prevention guidance cautions about ruck running. Knapik's review discusses load-carriage injuries and conditioning. These support taking progression seriously; they aren't trials of GORUCK's shuffle.

We haven't tested the shuffle against jogging. This is an analysis of GORUCK's published advice, the airborne shuffle studies and related load-carriage research, not an injury-prevention trial or a medical recommendation.

Should you try it?

For general fitness, brisk loaded walking remains a useful session on its own. Shuffling is an optional addition to that training. If you're training for a particular event, check its rules and practice the movement it requires.

If you're an experienced rucker interested in shuffling, start by understanding GORUCK's demonstration. Use less than your usual comfortable walking load, try a brief section on even ground, and reassess before extending it. That's a cautious way to explore a new movement, not a validated progression or a guarantee against injury.

Stop if you develop pain, numbness or an altered gait. Don't use a pace target to push through those symptoms. If you're new to rucking, build comfortable walking first; our beginner's guide is the better starting point.

Adjust the pack so the load stays close without restricting breathing or causing tingling. Less pack movement can improve how the setup feels, but it doesn't establish lower joint forces or make a particular weight safe.
