Rucking and running can both earn a place in a fitness routine. Running is the obvious choice if you want to run a faster race. Rucking gives you practice carrying a load while walking and can make a familiar route more demanding. For weight loss, neither gets to ignore what happens during the rest of the week.

Choose around the training goal

You can adjust a ruck's pace, terrain and load. You can adjust a run's pace and duration, or alternate running with walking. A pack doesn't automatically place someone in Zone 2, and unloaded walking can be sufficient exercise for someone starting out.

Comparing estimated calorie burn

These are gross energy estimates, including the energy you'd expend at rest. The walking rows use the Pandolf equation; the running row uses the ACSM running equation. They compare specified sessions, not the fitness or effort level of every person doing them. Use the calorie calculator to explore the assumptions.

Looney and colleagues' 2022 paper developed a metabolic model for heavy backpacking. It isn't a long-term trial comparing running and rucking for weight loss or knee injuries. Don't turn a calorie estimate into evidence for those outcomes.

A cast-iron ruck plate is one loading option, provided its dimensions and restraint suit the pack. The load should fit the session you can manage; it doesn't need to chase a calorie target.

Weight loss depends on more than the session

The higher-calorie session on paper won't necessarily produce more weight loss in practice. How often you do it, food intake, recovery and changes in activity outside exercise all affect the result. Research on exercise and energy compensation shows substantial variation between people.

Carrying weight adds muscular demand, but the studies cited here don't show that rucking preserves muscle better than running during weight loss. Resistance training and adequate nutrition belong in that discussion. Nor do we have evidence here for a universal advantage in adherence, burnout or dropout rates.

Impact and injury are different questions

Walking and running have different mechanics, and adding a pack changes walking's demands. Ground reaction forces, forces inside a joint and injuries over months are different measurements. A lower number for one doesn't automatically establish a lower injury rate.

Knapik and colleagues' load-carriage review describes injuries associated with carrying loads. It doesn't establish that recreational rucking causes half as many injuries as running. Both activities deserve a gradual introduction and attention to symptoms. Stop and get assessed if pain changes how you walk or run, or if you notice swelling, numbness, chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness. If you're pregnant, have a heart or metabolic condition, or are managing a diagnosed injury, check with a clinician before adding load or speed.

If shoes or clothing rub, address the fit early. Our footwear guide and Body Glide and foot-care coverage can help with equipment choices. Shoes and anti-chafe products aren't guarantees against injury.

Combining the two

If you already tolerate both activities, you might keep a ruck for loaded-walking practice and a run for running fitness. The balance depends on what you're preparing for. There isn't one optimal split for fat loss.

Avoid adding several hard sessions at once. Change one part of the schedule, see how you recover, and adjust before increasing more. The weight, distance and effort of a session matter as much as its label.
