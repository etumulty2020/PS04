The Short Answer

You can start a ruck club with just you and one other person. Pick a regular time, a route and a name, then show up.

A club can start with two people who meet every week. Consistency matters more than size or presentation.

Check if one already exists first. Browse our Ruck Club Finder, a directory of clubs in more than 35 countries with a map view and country and state filters. If there is already a club in your city, join that one. If not, this guide is for you.

Why Start a Ruck Club

The Accountability Effect

Rucking with a regular group can improve accountability and make the activity more social.

A fixed time with people expecting you adds a reason to show up beyond willpower. A 6 AM Tuesday ruck is harder to skip when four other people are waiting at the trailhead.

Community Over Isolation

Some people prefer to ruck alone. Others find a weekly walk easier to keep up with company. A club turns a solitary walk into a shared one, where members talk through the week, mark milestones and get to know each other outside the ruck.

An informal club can start with little overhead

The informal club described here does not charge a membership fee or employ coaches. Members agree on a public meeting place and ruck together. Local permit, insurance, and legal requirements still depend on where you live and how the group operates.

How Many People You Need to Start

Two.

You and one other person committed to showing up every week for the next 2 months. That is your ruck club.

Do not wait for 8 people. Do not wait for a full roster. Start with your friend, your coworker, your neighbor, or someone you meet online in the rucking community. If you don't know any ruckers yet, scan the Ruck Club Finder for clubs near you and reach out. Even a club an hour away may know one or two people in your own city who are looking for the same thing. Find one person and commit to a time.

Growth is not automatic, and it does not need to be fast. Start with the people who attend reliably, then make it easy for them to invite someone else.

A purpose-driven first event helps that growth along. A Memorial Day ruck gives a new club a clear purpose and a reason for strangers to show up.

Picking a Time and Route

Make it the same every week.

Pick a day and time and keep it consistent. A fixed weekly schedule makes attendance easier and gives the group time to build a habit.

Examples of working time slots:

Tuesday and Thursday 6 AM before work

Sunday 9 AM weekend warrior style

Wednesday 6 PM after work

Saturday morning long rucks

Your route should be:

Repeatable: the same loop every week so people know what they are signing up for

Accessible: starts and ends at a place with parking

Adjustable: sized to fit the session time, with an easy shortcut back to the start for anyone who wants less

Safe: well lit if early morning, with good sidewalks or paths and little car traffic

Start at a local park or parking lot. Map out your loop on Google Maps. Test it once. Then commit to it.

Naming Your Club

Pick something that reflects your personality and your place. "Portland Ruck Club" works. "Foggy City Ruckers" works. "Tuesday Morning Trail Warriors" works.

Keep it simple. Avoid inside jokes nobody else will understand. Your name should make sense to a stranger who finds you on Instagram.

Promote the club online

Create an Instagram account for your club in the first week. People can find the club through city searches, hashtags, and posts shared by current members.

What to post:

Before and after photos from your weekly rucks

Route maps and distance markers

New members joining

Callouts for upcoming sessions

Stories from events

Use hashtags: #RuckClub, #YourCity, #RuckersCommunity, #LoadedWalking, #GoRuck, and local hashtags like #PortlandFitness.

Post once per week minimum. Consistency here matters too. When someone scrolls looking for their local ruck club, they want to see recent activity.

Growing Through Word of Mouth

Your best marketing is people showing up and loving it. When people experience your club, they tell their friends. Their friends tell other friends.

To accelerate this:

Ask people to bring someone new. Every 2 to 3 weeks, have one member invite a friend.

Post in local fitness Facebook groups, just once, with your club name, time, location and Instagram handle.

Show up at local races and fitness events and hand out cards with your club details.

Connect with CrossFit gyms and running clubs, where some people are looking for a new outlet.

Do not hard sell. Just show up, be friendly, and let the experience speak for itself.

Gear Lending for Newcomers

One barrier you can remove is the newcomer who says "I don't have a ruck."

Buy two or three used packs that can hold a light load securely and keep them in your car. Lend them for free. Tell every new person "borrow this for 2 to 3 weeks, see if you like it, then get your own if you want."

Loaner gear removes the need for a newcomer to buy a pack before attending a first ruck.

Packs that work for lending include a used GORUCK GR1, the 5.11 Tactical RUSH 24 or a simple daypack that holds the load high and close. Fit and a secure place for the weight matter more than brand.

Structuring Your Sessions

Keep it simple. Every session follows this pattern:

Warm-up (5 to 10 minutes)

Dynamic stretching, arm circles, walking in place

Get people talking and loose

The Ruck (20 to 45 minutes)

Follow your route

Keep a conversational pace, so people can talk while walking

No one gets left behind

Cool-down (5 to 10 minutes)

Slow the pace in the last half mile

Static stretching at the end

Drink water

Optional Social (15 to 30 minutes)

Coffee, breakfast or conversation

Optional social time can help members get to know one another and return for future rucks.

Managing Mixed Fitness Levels

Expect a mix of beginners, experienced ruckers and people training for events.

The rule is simple: Everyone carries their own weight.

A beginner might carry 5 to 10 lb or nothing at all, while an experienced rucker carries more. Everyone walks the same route at the same conversational pace, and each person sets their own challenge through the load they choose.

Set the group pace at a conversational pace the slowest member can hold while talking. Nobody is racing.

Liability Considerations

Treat safety and legal structure as separate questions. Before the first public meetup:

Walk the route and identify road crossings, poor lighting, water access, and places where someone can leave early.

Collect an emergency contact and make sure at least one organizer carries a phone and a basic first-aid kit.

Tell participants the planned distance, pace, terrain, and whether weight is optional. Encourage them to stop if they feel pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath.

Check whether the park, trail, or private property requires a group permit or advance permission.

Do not assume that an informal group is exempt from every requirement, or that a sentence such as "ruck at your own risk" resolves liability. License, permit, insurance, and waiver rules vary by location and by whether you collect fees, hire coaches, advertise to the public, or host larger events. In the United States, the Small Business Administration directs organizers to check state and local registration, permit, and insurance requirements. Outside the United States, check the equivalent local authority.

If the club collects money, uses paid staff, runs events, or grows beyond a small informal meetup, speak with a local insurer and qualified lawyer about the right structure and participant documents. This guide is practical planning information, not legal advice.

Events. A local GORUCK event or charity ruck gives members a shared goal to train for together.

Online communities. Forums such as r/rucking on Reddit are places to tell people about your club and to help others start one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my club is "working"?

You know your club is working when people show up without being reminded. When they bring friends. When they ask "same time next week?" at the end of a session. When they text you during the week saying they are thinking about the ruck.

Judge success by consistency. Four people who show up every week give a club more to build on than twelve who come occasionally.

What if people stop showing up?

This happens. People move, get injured, discover it is not for them. Do not take it personally.

If at least two people still meet, the club can continue. If not, pause for a month and ask core members whether they want to restart. Membership changes over time, and a small core can carry the club through it.

Can I charge a membership fee?

You can, but you probably should not. Fees add accounting, liability, and new member expectations.

Keep it free. Your investment is your time. That is enough.

If you want to cover costs (parking pass, club t-shirts, or group lunches), ask for voluntary contributions. Never mandate payment.

What if someone gets injured during a ruck?

Stay calm, assess the injury, stop the session if needed, and call 911 if it is serious.

Carry a phone and basic first-aid kit, and know the route's access points so emergency services can reach you.

If someone does get hurt during a club session, be supportive, encourage them to see a doctor, and let them know the club is here when they recover. Afterward, note what happened and review the route and plan. If the club has insurance, follow its reporting steps.

How do I find ruck clubs near me?

Start with our Ruck Club Finder, a global directory with a map view, country and state filters, and clubs in more than 35 countries. Search by your city or browse the map. Cross-check GORUCK's own club list for any clubs we haven't picked up yet.

If neither directory has one nearby, search Instagram for #YourCity + #RuckClub. Post in local running groups asking if anyone rucks. Ask in CrossFit gyms.

If you still cannot find one after 2 weeks of searching, that is your signal to start one yourself, then list it on our directory so the next person searching finds you.
