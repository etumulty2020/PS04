The short answer

Rucking isn't meditation, but it shares some of its effects. You move, carry weight, breathe rhythmically, and spend time outside, and each of those has been linked to better mental health in studies. Together, they make a low-barrier way into physical activity that many people find easy to stick with.

This article is written for people dealing with anxiety, stress, or low mood. It is not a replacement for professional treatment; therapists, psychiatrists, and counselors exist for good reasons. But adding regular activity like rucking to whatever else you are doing (therapy, medication, lifestyle changes) is a reasonable, well-supported step.

Why rucking, specifically, for mental health?

Most fitness marketing promises immediate mood boosts, and real life is messier than that. The case for rucking rests on three separate mechanisms, each with research behind it.

Mechanism one: aerobic exercise helps depression

Exercise is one of the few non-drug interventions that has held up against medication in a randomized trial.

Blumenthal et al. (2007) studied adults with major depressive disorder and found that a structured aerobic exercise program (brisk walking or jogging three times per week for 16 weeks) produced remission rates similar to sertraline, a common antidepressant.

Proposed reasons include better sleep, changes in brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and inflammation, and shifts in neurotransmitter activity, along with the sense of accomplishment that comes from doing something hard. Steady rucking is one practical way to get regular aerobic exercise.

Blumenthal et al. (2007) randomized 202 adults with major depressive disorder to supervised group exercise, home-based exercise, sertraline, or a placebo pill for 16 weeks. Remission rates were 45% for supervised exercise, 40% for home-based exercise, 47% for sertraline and 31% for placebo. The authors judged exercise generally comparable to medication, while noting that the high placebo response meant expectations and attention explained a good part of the improvement in every group.

Mechanism two: outdoor time reduces rumination

Rumination, the loop of repetitive, negative thought, is a core feature of anxiety and depression. Walking in nature appears to interrupt it.

Bratman et al. (2015) sent healthy adults on either a 90-minute walk in a natural setting or a 90-minute walk along a busy urban street. The nature walkers reported less rumination afterward and showed reduced activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex, a brain region linked to rumination. The urban walkers showed neither change.

Rucking outside, on trails or through parks, puts you in the kind of setting Bratman tested. The pack weight and the attention needed to hold your pace and footing give your mind something concrete to do.

Bratman et al. (2015) scanned participants' brains before and after the walks. The nature walk reduced both self-reported rumination and activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex; the urban walk did not. The study was small and in healthy adults, but it points to one pathway by which time in nature may support mental well-being.

Mechanism three: rhythmic, load-bearing movement is grounding

The load on your back and the steady rhythm of your footfall give your body constant feedback about where it is in space, what occupational therapists call proprioceptive input. Some people find deep pressure calming, which is part of the appeal of weighted blankets, although research on those is mixed and no one has studied whether a pack has a similar effect.

Walking at a steady pace also tends to settle your breathing into a rhythm with your steps. Slow, even breathing is associated with higher heart rate variability, a marker of a more relaxed nervous system. You move, your body feels the load, you breathe in rhythm, and for many people that is enough to take the edge off high alert.

Rucking for anxiety and stress

Anxiety tends to live in the future, in things that have not happened yet. Rucking pulls your attention back to the present.

When you ruck, you feel:

The pack weight on your shoulders

Your feet hitting the ground

Your breathing pace matching your steps

The air temperature on your skin

The terrain changing beneath you

Therapists call deliberately engaging your senses to interrupt anxious thinking "grounding," and a ruck builds it into a walk.

Many people find that a single walk takes the edge off anxiety for a while afterward. Rucking at a moderate, conversational effort is sustainable and repeatable, which matters because the habit is what builds up over weeks and months.

If hard, all-out workouts leave you feeling more wired than calm, a conversational-pace ruck is a gentler alternative.

Rucking for depression

Depression often brings low energy and low motivation along with low mood, which makes the activation barrier high. Getting off the couch can feel impossible.

Rucking can help here because the barrier to entry is lower than running or CrossFit. You do not need speed or intensity. You do not need to compete. You do not need to feel good first. You walk while carrying some weight.

The evidence for exercise and depression is broad. A meta-analysis by Rethorst et al. (2009) pooled 58 randomized trials with nearly 3,000 participants and found that people who exercised had substantially lower depression scores than controls, with dropout rates similar to those seen in psychotherapy and drug trials.

Rucking adds a strength element to walking, and staying physically active helps preserve fitness and muscle during periods when motivation is low.

The moving meditation angle

Rucking is not meditation in the traditional sense. You are not sitting still, and you are not trying to empty your mind.

But it can produce something close to what psychologists call "flow," a state where your attention is fully absorbed by what you're doing. Your to-do list fades because your brain is occupied with:

Balancing the load

Matching your pace

Breathing

Navigating the terrain

Walking also seems to free up thinking. Oppezzo & Schwartz (2014) found that walking, even on a treadmill facing a wall, boosted creative idea generation compared with sitting, and in one of their experiments walking outdoors produced the most novel ideas. Many ruckers report that problems feel more manageable at the end of a walk than at the start.

The community and accountability factor

Social connection is one of the most underrated mental health tools, and group rucking builds community without much competitive pressure.

Running clubs can sort people by pace, and some gyms make intensity the price of admission. Rucking groups usually move at a conversational pace. You can ruck in a group and actually talk. You see the same people weekly and build friendships around a shared activity.

For people with depression or social anxiety, a weekly group ruck can become a reliable social commitment. The activity is low-pressure enough that showing up is possible, even on hard days, and showing up consistently is how connection happens.

Loneliness is closely tied to depression and anxiety, and rucking groups, intentionally or not, give people a regular reason to be around others.

Rucking for veterans mental health

Rucking can support veterans' mental health by combining familiar load-bearing movement, outdoor time, peer connection, and predictable structure. It is not a PTSD treatment, crisis intervention, or substitute for therapy, but it can be a practical bridge back into movement and community.

The clinical evidence for exercise as PTSD support is promising but early. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis in Federal Practitioner, Reis et al., Exercise to Reduce Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Symptoms in Veterans (PMC9217079), pooled three randomized trials with 217 veterans and found a small-to-medium reduction in PTSD symptoms that did not reach statistical significance. The authors called it preliminary evidence that exercise may be a useful option and asked for more high-quality trials.

The setting may matter too. A pilot randomized trial in the Pacific Northwest, Littman et al. (2021), nature versus urban hiking for Veterans with PTSD (PMC8461737), assigned 26 veterans to six group hikes in nature or in urban settings over 12 weeks. PTSD symptom scores improved more in the nature group at 12 and 24 weeks, but the pilot was designed to test feasibility, so the difference was not tested for significance. It echoes Bratman's findings in non-veteran adults, with a larger trial still needed.

Post-traumatic stress, military sexual trauma, moral injury, grief, and the transition out of military service create specific mental health challenges. Rucking helps most when it is framed as structured movement with people, not as a toughness test.

Groups like Team Red White & Blue and local veteran rucking clubs have built communities around this explicitly. If you are a veteran looking for mental health support, rucking can be useful as a gateway habit: show up, walk at a conversational pace, build trust, then use that stability to support deeper work with a trauma-informed therapist or VA care team.

If you are a Veteran in crisis or concerned about one, the Veterans Crisis Line offers free, confidential support 24/7. Dial 988 then press 1, chat online, or text 838255. You do not have to be enrolled in VA benefits or health care to connect.

What rucking is not

To be clear about what this article does not claim:

Rucking is not a replacement for therapy or medication. If you are struggling with depression or anxiety, see a licensed therapist or psychiatrist. Physical activity is a complement, not a substitute.

Rucking will not instantly fix your mood. Mental health improvements usually show up over weeks and months, not days. You may not feel better after your first ruck, and that is normal.

Rucking will not "fix" you. Mental health is complex. Rucking changes one variable: physical activity. Sleep, nutrition, social connection, meaningful work, and a sense of purpose matter too. Treat rucking as one tool in a larger toolkit.

Not everyone will like rucking. Some people prefer running, cycling, swimming, or climbing. The best exercise for mental health is the one you will actually do. Rucking isn't mystically superior; it's just accessible.

Getting started: the minimal viable ruck

You do not need expensive gear to ruck for mental health, and you do not need a heavy pack.

Start with:

A backpack you already own, ideally structured and comfortable (or check out our budget rucking starter kit for affordable options)

5 to 10 lbs of weight for your first sessions: water bottles, books, a sandbag, or later a single ruck plate like the ones in our ruck plate guide

Shoes you can walk in; no special running shoes required

20 to 30 minutes at a conversational pace

A local trail or park, because outdoor time matters

That is it. Walk at a sustainable pace. Bring your phone to listen to a podcast or audiobook if that helps with engagement, or walk in silence. Both work.

Aim for three rucks per week if you can. Two is fine. One is better than zero. Consistency matters more than intensity.
