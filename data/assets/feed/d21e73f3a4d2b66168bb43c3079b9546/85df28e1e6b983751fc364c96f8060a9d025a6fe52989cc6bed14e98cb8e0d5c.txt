How should winter boots fit?

Finding the right fit can be tricky. Keep these tips in mind:

Test with different socks:

Try boots with thick socks (like wool) and thinner socks (like ski socks) to ensure flexibility in fit.

Allow some wiggle room:

Boots should not feel tight, but avoid too much room to prevent slipping. Walk around to check for rubbing heels, cramped toes, or inadequate ankle support.

Consider wide feet options:

Look for wide models if needed. Some brands, such as the SOREL boots, tend to run large, offering more room.

What to look for in winter boots

Warmth

Choose boots with insulation that matches your winter conditions. Look for materials like wool or Thinsulate for extreme cold, and lighter options if you're active in milder temperatures. Ensure your boots keep your feet warm without overheating.

Tread and traction

Opt for boots with deep treads if you expect icy or snowy terrain. Specialized winter boots offer better grip than hiking boots, which helps prevent slipping on wet or packed snow. For extra grip on ice, you can also add traction devices over your boots.

Waterproofing

Waterproof boots are essential for snow and slush. Look for sealed seams and waterproof membranes to keep moisture out. Consider boots with removable liners to manage sweat and prevent odor buildup over time.

Fit and comfort

Fit varies by brand, so test your boots with different sock thicknesses to find the right balance. If you have wide feet, seek models with wider options. Comfort matters—walk in the boots to ensure no rubbing or discomfort.

Height

Decide between ankle-height and mid-calf boots based on your needs. Higher boots offer better protection from snow, while shorter ones can feel lighter and more versatile for everyday wear.

Weight

Heavier boots provide more insulation and traction but can tire your feet faster. Consider your activity level and how much weight you’re comfortable carrying for extended periods.

Breathability

Breathability often competes with warmth and waterproofing. If you’re prone to sweaty feet, look for boots with moisture-wicking linings or materials that balance warmth and ventilation.

Insulation Types

Different insulation materials suit different climates. Thick wool or synthetic insulation works best for freezing temps, while lighter materials suffice for milder winters. Choose the right insulation based on your activities and weather conditions.

How to choose the right winter boots for your needs

Finding the perfect pair of winter boots starts with considering where you’ll wear them and what you’ll be doing. Whether you’re braving city slush or trekking through rural snowdrifts, here’s how to choose boots that meet your needs.

For urban winters

If you’re commuting, running errands, or navigating icy sidewalks, focus on:

Waterproofing: Keep your feet dry in rain, snow, or slush with waterproof materials and sealed seams.

Grip: Sidewalks and stairs can be slippery, so look for soles with excellent traction.

Style: Winter boots can still look great! Leather or faux-fur-trimmed designs balance fashion and functionality.

Light insulation: Opt for 200 g insulation to keep your feet warm without overheating during short outdoor stints.

For rural winters

Living in a snowy, rural area, working on a farm, and getting out into the woods often means dealing with deeper snow, muddier trails, and harsher conditions. Choose boots that prioritize:

Warmth: Go for thicker insulation (400–600g or more) for all-day warmth, especially if you’re spending extended time outside.

Tall shafts: Keep snow out with boots that rise higher on your calves.

Durability: Reinforced materials and rugged soles handle tougher terrain and frequent use.

Weatherproofing: Waterproof uppers and outsoles are non-negotiable for staying dry.

For hiking or snowshoeing

If you’re exploring trails or taking on snowshoe adventures:

Ankle support: A snug fit and sturdy build help stabilize your footing on uneven terrain.

Traction: Tread patterns designed for snow or compatibility with ice cleats are key for grip.

Waterproofing: Snow can creep in—pairing boots with gaiters is a smart move.

If you want to wear your boots with snowshoes, consider something with more support.

If you are mostly hiking, you may be able to get away with some hiking boots or even lightweight hiking boots depending on temperatures and conditions.

For more, see tips from our guide on what to wear winter hiking.

And learn basic snowshoeing skills before you head out.

For cold-weather travel

Heading out for a ski trip or visiting a colder climate?

Choose lightweight, packable boots that are easy to slip on and off.

Insulation and waterproofing are important, but you may not need heavy-duty options if your time outdoors is limited.

For outdoor work

Winter work boots should prioritize:

Ease of use: Slip-on options are convenient for quick tasks, while lace-ups provide extra support for long hours.

Durability: Reinforced toes and rugged soles stand up to wear and tear.

Warmth and waterproofing: Insulated, weatherproof designs are a must for staying comfortable.

Quick tips for common concerns:

Cold feet? Try boots with thicker insulation or a fleece lining for extra warmth.

Overheating? Opt for lightly insulated or non-insulated boots, especially for active use.

Deep snow? Look for taller shafts or add gaiters to your setup.

Wet conditions? Waterproof materials and sealed seams are your best defense.

Insulated vs non-insulated winter boots

It’s a much-asked question—do I need an insulated boot or will uninsulated waterproof boots work for me? Here are some pros and cons for each type of boot and our thoughts on the types of people and conditions where you may prefer one boot over the other.

Why choose insulated winter boots?

If your feet are always cold, we're here to help find a boot that can fix that with the right boot choices.

Insulation like Thinsulate, PrimaLoft, shearling, fleece, and neoprene can ensure your toes stay warm. Even if you don’t want to overheat your feet, your toes are one of the furthest extremities from your heart, and they need a little help to stay warmer in extreme temperatures.

In an insulated boot, look for thick soles that keep your feet away from the snow and ice, and ruffs around the top to trap snow before it falls down towards your feet.

Why choose non-insulated winter boots?

Having spent time in Alaska, I’ve known cold. And I’ve known rain. Sometimes those two things coincided, with 40°F deluges instead of nice fluffy snow at 30°F weather. Sometimes, all I need is a winter boot that will keep my toes moderately warm but will really keep the water, snow, and slush out. 99% of the time — including shoveling, skiing, hiking, snowmobiling, walking, and working — I wear uninsulated rubber boots throughout the winter.

If you’re like me, you might tend to run warm, and nothing’s worse for me than walking in from a 15°F day to an 80-degree waiting room. I appreciate winter for the cold, not for being hot. So on an average day, I can’t stand to wear insulated boots because I’ll simply be too hot running errands, going to work, or going out to eat. Even with uninsulated boots, I still have a degree of warmth and a healthy degree of waterproofness that won’t make my feet feel like they’re swimming in a sauna.

Living in Colorado’s Front Range, insulated boots are overkill for me for all but 3 days out of the year. Since the Front Range tends to run warm — a recent 45°F, sunny day had me in a tank top, after all — I tend to choose my footwear based on their effectiveness throughout the different seasons. I can wear an uninsulated pair of boots throughout the fall and spring, as well as in the dead of winter. How can I do this? It all comes down to the socks that I wear with them.

On days above freezing, I can wear thinner socks, while frigid days have me pulling out my thick wool socks. As long as you find a pair of uninsulated boots that can accommodate different thicknesses of socks, you’ll have a pair of boots that prove to be far more versatile across a range of temperatures and conditions.

What socks should I wear with my winter boots?

Warm ones! Seriously, though, there are so many sock options to choose from that we’ll stick to the basics — find warm socks that fit well (not too loose and not too tight) that will still fit into your boots. While I’m a big fan of ski socks for most of my winter activities, both hiking socks and ski socks have served me well in subzero temperatures when paired with a good winter boot.﻿

Care and maintenance

Winter boots take a beating from snow, slush, mud, and road salt. How you care for them depends on what they’re made of, so check the manufacturer’s instructions before washing, treating, or waterproofing your boots. Leather, suede, rubber, wool, and waterproof membranes don’t all need the same treatment.

Cleaning and drying

Brush or wipe off dirt, mud, and salt after use rather than letting it build up. A damp cloth is enough for many boots, but use a cleaner made for the material if they need a deeper clean.

If your boots get wet, remove the insoles or removable liners and let everything air dry completely. Avoid putting boots in a dryer or directly against a radiator or other strong heat source unless the manufacturer specifically says it’s safe.

Maintaining waterproofing

If water stops beading on the surface or the outer material begins wetting through, it may be time to restore the boot’s water repellency. Use a treatment designed for that specific material and follow the product and boot manufacturer’s instructions.

That last part matters. A waterproofing treatment that works well on one pair of leather boots may be completely wrong for suede, fabric, rubber, or a membrane-lined boot.

Soles, insoles, and other wear

Check the soles periodically for worn tread, especially if you rely on the boots for traction on snow or ice. Some boots can be resoled, while others can’t, so check with the manufacturer or a cobbler before assuming a worn outsole means the whole boot is finished.

Insoles can also wear down before the rest of the boot. If they’ve lost their cushioning or support, replacing them may make an otherwise good pair more comfortable again.

Storage

When the snow melts and it’s time to start thinking about flip-flops, clean your boots and let them dry completely before putting them away. Storing damp boots is an easy way to end up with mildew, odor, or damaged materials by next winter.

Ideally, store them somewhere cool and dry where they won’t get crushed. If you’re like me and don’t exactly have your gear storage on lock, under the bed works too. The important part is making sure they’re clean and completely dry first.

FAQ

Can I use hiking boots in the winter?

Sometimes. A waterproof hiking boot can work well in mild winter conditions, especially if you’re moving and don’t need much insulation. I’ve worn hiking boots in winter plenty of times when the temperatures and snow conditions made sense.

Where they start to fall short is deep snow, serious cold, or long periods standing around outside. Many hiking boots aren’t tall enough to keep snow out and don’t have the insulation of a true winter boot. If you mostly hike in warmer months and only occasionally get out in winter, though, you may not need a separate heavy winter hiking boot.

Are all winter boots waterproof?

No. Some winter boots are fully waterproof, some are water-resistant, and others are mainly designed for warmth.

Think about the kind of winter you actually deal with. If your winters mean slush, wet snow, puddles, or rain, waterproofing matters a lot. If you live somewhere colder and drier, warmth and insulation may be the bigger priority.

What’s the difference between leather and synthetic winter boots?

Both can work well. Leather is durable, can mold nicely to your feet over time, and is common in boots that are meant to last for years. It may need more care, depending on the type of leather and how the boot is waterproofed.

Synthetic boots are often lighter and may require less maintenance. They’re also common in winter hiking boots where manufacturers are trying to balance warmth, waterproofing, and weight.

I wouldn’t choose a winter boot based on the upper material alone. Fit, insulation, waterproofing, traction, and what you plan to use the boot for matter more.

Do winter boots need a break-in period?

Some do, especially heavier leather boots. Others feel comfortable right away.

A little stiffness at first can be normal, but a winter boot shouldn’t feel painfully tight or create obvious pressure points when you try it on. Remember that you may also be wearing thicker socks in winter, so make sure there’s enough room for those without your foot sliding around.

How do I keep winter boots from smelling?

The biggest thing is letting them dry completely between uses. Take out removable insoles or liners if they’re damp and let everything air out before wearing the boots again.

Moisture-wicking socks can also help. If odor becomes a recurring problem, check the manufacturer’s cleaning instructions before adding powders, sprays, or other treatments to the inside of the boot.
