The short answer

Rucking mainly builds muscular endurance and work capacity. Because it is submaximal, high-repetition loaded walking, it is less effective for hypertrophy, or increased muscle size, than progressive resistance training.

Beginners, deconditioned people, and some older adults may gain strength and a modest amount of muscle because the load is a new stimulus. A trained lifter is more likely to gain endurance than size from rucking alone.

What Rucking Trains

When you walk under load, a lot of your body is working, even though it feels like "just walking."

Your quads, hamstrings, glutes, and calves drive each step and control your descent, especially downhill. Your core and spinal erectors work continuously to keep you upright against a load that's trying to pull you backward and sideways. Your traps, rhomboids, and shoulders stabilize the pack. Even your feet and lower legs (including the tibialis) get a steady training effect from the extra weight.

The main demand falls on the muscles used for walking, posture, and pack control rather than on the arms or chest.

A 2012 systematic review of load-carriage training by Knapik and colleagues found the largest improvements in loaded-march performance when progressive load carriage was combined with resistance and aerobic training. That evidence is about carrying performance and load tolerance. It doesn't show large increases in muscle size from loaded walking.

Why rucking isn't a great hypertrophy tool

To understand why rucking doesn't build big muscles, it helps to know what does.

Muscle growth is driven mainly by mechanical tension: challenging a muscle with enough load, through enough range, close enough to failure, and then progressively adding more over time. That's why lifting works. You take a muscle near its limit for a handful of reps, then add weight next month.

Rucking provides many submaximal repetitions rather than sets taken close to muscular failure. Pack weight can be progressed, but it cannot target a specific muscle as precisely as a squat, row, or hip thrust. That makes it a less efficient way to increase muscle size.

Endurance-style, high-repetition work tends to produce endurance adaptations. If maximum muscle size is the goal, use progressive resistance training as the primary method.

What Rucking Can Do for Muscle

It adds a loading stimulus. Weight loss can reduce lean mass as well as fat. Rucking provides some weight-bearing resistance, though resistance training and adequate protein have stronger direct support for muscle preservation. This distinction matters for people rucking on GLP-1 medications.

It builds work capacity and strength endurance. The ability to carry, climb, and keep moving under load can transfer to hiking and some daily carrying tasks.

It trains postural endurance. The trunk, spinal erectors, and upper back work throughout the walk. Rucking can strengthen those muscles, but it can also aggravate back pain when the load, fit, or progression is wrong. See is rucking bad for your back.

It loads bone. Rucking is weight-bearing and usually lower impact than running, but the external load still increases stress on the feet, joints, and spine. It is not risk-free.

Who will build visible muscle from rucking alone

Whether rucking noticeably grows your muscles depends heavily on where you're starting.

Muscle growth is more plausible if you are new to exercise, returning after a long layoff, or starting from a low activity level. Even then, the amount is uncertain. Anyone returning from injury should use a clinician-approved plan rather than assuming loaded walking is appropriate.

If you already lift or train regularly, rucking is less likely to add noticeable size. It may improve muscular endurance, but it should not be assumed to maintain all muscle during a calorie deficit.

If rucking is your main strength stimulus, start light. Add load or hills only when form and recovery are stable, and include simple resistance training if greater strength or muscle size is the goal.

How to make your rucking more muscle-building

You can nudge rucking toward more of a strength stimulus. It won't turn into a leg day, but these tweaks meaningfully raise the muscular demand.

Add hills and stairs. Incline raises the demand on the glutes, hamstrings, and quads, while descents add braking work. Our hill ruck training guide has structured sessions.

Go heavier for shorter walks. A heavier load over 20 to 30 minutes shifts the stimulus toward strength; a light load over two hours shifts it toward endurance. Progress load in small steps. Ruck plates usually step up in 10 lb jumps, so smaller weights can fill the gap between them.

Build in loaded carries and circuits. Stop mid-ruck for walking lunges, step-ups, or bodyweight squats with the pack on. Use controlled form and choose repetitions that leave room for the walk home.

Pair it with resistance training. Two weekly sessions are a practical starting point when muscle growth is the goal. See rucking and lifting for scheduling options.

Your next step

Match the tool to your goal. If you want to get bigger and stronger, make lifting the centerpiece and use rucking for conditioning. If you want durability and work capacity, rucking can do a lot of that on its own, with hills and a little weight added over time. Add some resistance training if keeping muscle is a priority.
