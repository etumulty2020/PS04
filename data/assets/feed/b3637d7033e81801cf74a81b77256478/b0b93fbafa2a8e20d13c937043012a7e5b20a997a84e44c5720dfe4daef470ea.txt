A backcountry hunter’s backpack is an indispensable tool. It serves as pantry, dresser, optics case, first aid cabinet, seat, and so many other things. The best hunting backpacks do all of those things, but also haul that precious cargo of meat out after the tags are punched.

Way back in 2011, I used the original KUIU Icon frame and bag and appreciated the quality craftsmanship and clever organization. The carbon frame was innovative and did a good job with moderate loads. Under really heavy loads, though, it got overwhelmed, and some hunters had durability issues, including frames cracking and breaking. KUIU went through more iterations and improvements, always preserving their organizational prowess but improving fit and finish. Performance did improve, and many hunters really found the newer Icon Pro and Pro LT to be a good fit for their hunts. I liked the weight and function of the newer packs, but still felt heavy loads got too uncomfortable, and the suspension just didn’t quite fit me right.

Significantly improve heavy-weight carrying capacity and comfort.

Simplify the overall design while dramatically improving performance.

To reach these goals, KUIU started from scratch, which means the new Icon 6 packs and frames are not backward compatible with the older Icons, including the Pro and Pro LT models.

It all starts with the new patent-pending frame, which combines a “corrugated carbon architecture” made of thermoplastic carbon fiber. The new frame is taller and wider than previous KUIU frames and is specifically designed to flex where it is needed and stay stiff for load-bearing. The new frame promises increased strength and durability as well as improved “damage tolerance.” KUIU is confident the new frame satisfies design goal #1.

The suspension fits through hook-and-loop attachment and offers excellent adjustability in both length and angle. The hip belt and shoulder straps have been redesigned in shape and materials, with new foam mapped to be thinner and thicker depending on anatomic need. A thick lumbar pad matches a new wider hip belt.

The available pack bags include the tested 3500 CI daypack, a versatile 4500 CI option, a multi-day 6000 CI freighter, and an expedition-ready 8200 CI behemoth. Quality 330 denier Cordura and Waterproof Cordura are the primary fabrics used in the construction of the packs. My 3500 test pack is well made with quality stitching and materials throughout. Packs attach to the frame simply by placing the packbag’s hypalon stay pockets over the top stays and tensioning by way of two hook-and-loop straps under the frame by the lumbar pad.

Design goal #2 was design simplicity. This goal speaks to me because some of my favorite backpacks have high performance and function, but take a lot of time and effort to do things like use the load shelf or switch bags. Here is a huge win for KUIU, as this is one of the simplest designs in a hunting pack.

To use the load sling the hunter just needs to unbuckle the compression strap bucks, detach the bag attachment straps and lift the hypalon frame stay pockets off the upper frame stays. The top straps will squeeze through the frame stay pockets without removing the buckles from the straps. Switching pack bags is a breeze. The Icon 6 doesn’t have load stabilizer straps, so there is nothing to unthread when removing the pack, attaching the pack, or loading the sling.

In typical KUIU fashion, the 3500 pack has plenty of storage options. The main compartment access is a full u-shaped zipper, so the hunter can position the zippers for easy top or side access. The main compartment has a zippered mesh long side pocket on one side and an open sleeve pocket on the other side. I liked the sleeve for my preferred hard side hydration water bottle. Bladder users have a traditional bladder pocket with hanging strap. This pocket will fit really large water bladders.

The outer panel has a large zippered mesh pocket on the inside and two faux-lid pockets on the outside. This is a convenient alternative to a traditional pocketed lid. Under those pockets is a tall pocket on the outer panel which makes for quick use items like rain gear or sundries, but is also long enough to fit a full size spotting scope. There are two traditional open top pockets on each exterior side of the main bag for trekking poles, tripods, or additional water bottles. There are two hypalon handles on the back panel which are a nice touch for moving around the loaded pack.

There are two removable hip belt pockets as well. These pockets can be easily removed to attach other accessories to the underlying webbing. The storage is ample for a day hunt with lots of gear, but I also easily had enough room for overnight scouting trips.

Loads are secured to the frame via eight main compression straps. Two top straps attached to the top stays, two bottom straps and two side straps on each side. The side straps on the driver’s side are female buckles and the passenger sides are male. This makes it easy to snap them together for loading the frame without a pack or compressing items to the exterior of the pack.

Two compression straps with t-connections can be used on the main back panel daisy chains. These were a kind of mystery to me, because it seems the main side compression straps can do the same job, only better. If you use the daisy chain straps, you don’t have a hard point of contact to pull against, and are just pulling against the fabric of the bag. I tried mounting my bow to these straps, but it just wasn’t as secure as I liked. I then used the top side compression straps together and was able to secure my bow nice and snug. I did use a lower daisy chain strap to keep the bottom riser close to the bag, but the lower side straps could have done the job as well. I actually plan to leave the daisy chain straps at home moving forward.

The main compression strap system does a great job of keeping the load secure whether using the frame without a pack, with the load hauler, with the bag in load sling mode, and with the pack alone. The side strap buckles have lever tensioners to prevent loosening while the top and bottom strap have strap locking tabs on the buckles. Both styles take an extra step to loosen compared to traditional buckles but I got accustomed to them in time.

A big plus for the Icon 6 system is the included accessories. Each full pack system (frame+bag) ordered comes with a quick-release rifle holder, two zippered hip belt pouches, a TPU accessory strap, and a Pack Load Hauler.

The Pack Load Hauler is pretty clever and serves double duty. It is a thinly padded glassing pad that also serves as a compression panel for using the Icon 6 frame as a freighter. It works great although sliding the bottom compression straps through the sleeves on the side is kind of tedious and doesn’t really fit the simple and quick ethos of the rest of the system. Still it absolutely works great once you get it installed, and I loved having a glassing pad at the ready.

The belt pockets are medium-sized, convenient, and one-hand operable. There is an underlying strap for other accessories, but I really like having them on the belt with my headlamp on one side and glassing accessories like an Outdoorsman’s bino adapter and digiscoping adapter on the other.

The rifle holder attaches to the side of the bag, and is designed to allow for rifle removal while wearing the bag. I have tried it, and it works. I need more practice and time with it to really get the hang of it, but my impression right now is that I prefer other rifle carry methods like those from Creative Outdoors or the Kifaru Gun Bearer.

The TPU strap can be attached to the side and the frame and used for a wide variety of things like an alternate method of securing a rifle, or attaching antlers, or any utility attachment use. That said, I haven’t actually found a preferred use for it at this point, personally.

I have spent a lot of time with KUIU packs. I have always liked a lot of their pack features, including the light weights, quality construction, and thoughtful organization. In fact, my most used scouting and trail maintenance pack is the KUIU Divide 2000. Its design and comfort with daypack loads is awesome, but obviously it is not meant for hauling meat.

The previous generation pack-on-frame Icons were certainly capable of hauling just about anything I could strap to them. However, they just didn’t carry those loads as comfortably as some of the top performers. For me, two of those top performers are the Stone Glacier XCurve frame (with Sky 5900 pack) and the Kifaru ARK frame (with Bedlam pack). For this review, I made 1.2-mile loops (510 vertical feet gained and lost) with each pack loaded with 28 pounds of day hunting gear, followed by re-hiking the same loop with an additional 64 pounds of blasting media in a game bag to simulate an elk quarter. Each loop included portions on trail, off trail, and through deadfall.

With the packs loaded with day gear, it was difficult to really find the difference in comfort. I had zero belt slippage, no hot spots, and no shoulder discomfort with any of these three pack systems. The ARK frame was the stiffest, while the Icon 6 flexed a little more with the body, and the Xcurve was pretty laterally compliant.

Of course, I fully expected bigger differences to emerge during the heavy load loops. I was pleasantly surprised by the KUIU Icon 6 in this testing. The Icon 6 belt is wider than previous Icon belts, and at 5.75 inches at its widest, it is about 0.25 inches wider than the Xcurve belt but still narrower than the 7.5-inch ARK belt. The load weight was evenly distributed around the Icon 6 belt, and the load lifters sufficiently pulled the load into a nice balance. During the loop, I cranked the belt tight and tensioned the shoulder straps just enough. The result was a very stable load that doesn’t make the hunter lean too far forward. Keeping with the simple ethos of the Icon 6, there are no “stabilizing straps” connecting the bottom of the pack to the belt, but they just weren’t needed. The new wider frame just snugs the pack to the frame solidly, with or without the load sling loaded.

These are some of the best frames available to hunters, and as such none of them caused me any discomfort on the hips or lumbar area during the heavy packout load testing. Like the daypack loads, the packout performance was pretty similar. The Icon 6 was dramatically better than prior Icon frames I have used. Earlier designs caused substantial hip/lumbar abrasion and tenderness, as well as shoulder pain and fatigue with heavy loads. It didn’t matter how I tried to adjust the suspension. The Icon 6 completely remedied this for me, and brings the Icon 6 on the level with the very best frames available. Compared to the Xcurve, the Icon 6 had a much stiffer feel, but still flexed a bit when crawling over downed logs. The ARK is stiffer laterally, and … while still very comfortable … has more of a packboard-type feel. As a personal preference, I preferred the feel of the Icon 6 and ARK to the Xcurve in the heavy load testing.

The Icon 6 comes in two frame sizes. The Regular frame is 24.5 inches and the Tall frame is 26.5 inches. There are specific men’s and women’s suspensions available for these frames. My test Regular frame just under 10 inches wide and lumbar pad and top stays.

I weighed the Icon 6 3500 with all the accessories I would actually use including both hip belt pockets and the Pack Load Hauler. The real world empty weight was 5 lbs. 12.5 ounces. This is in line with the Stone Glacier Sky 5900 (with belt pocket, lid, and water bottle holder) at 5 lbs. 13 ounces. The Kifaru ARK/Bedlam tips the scales over a pound heavier at exactly 7 lbs with a medium belt pocket and water bottle holder.

The Icon 6 3500 base system including accessories comes in at $599. This pricing puts it squarely in the premium pack market but lower than top competitors. The Stone Glacier Sky 5900 is $725 without any accessories. The Kifaru ARK with the Bedlam runs a base price of $850. The Icon 6 is a downright bargain in the premium pack area especially when accounting for the included accessories.

KUIU has elevated their Icon backpack line with their new Icon 6 frame and bags. The expressed design goals of improved heavy load comfort and simplified use have been satisfied. They have managed to maintain the low weights and organization they are known for while bringing the load-carrying performance and comfort up to compete with the very best in the market. It can not be overstated just how welcome and convenient a simple and intuitive design is for changing bags and converting for load hauling.

Everything isn’t perfect with the Icon 6. The all-new design of the Icon 6 leaves existing Icon/Icon LT users with an incompatible system. I don’t quite get the daisy chain straps, and the Pack Load Hauler is finicky to get on the straps. Even so, there is no denying the Icon 6 system is a big step forward for KUIU’s frame and pack system.
