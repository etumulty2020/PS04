Rucking vs Walking: The Short Answer

Rucking is walking with a weighted backpack. A 180-lb person walking at 3.5 mph on flat pavement burns about 365 calories an hour. Add a 25-lb pack and that rises to about 400, roughly 11 percent more in the same hour. Take the same pack up a 5 percent grade and it is about 620. These are model estimates, not measured personal expenditure.

The practical benefit: You can raise the effort of a walk without changing to running. That is useful if it suits your goal; ordinary walking doesn't need upgrading to count as exercise.

What the research says. The metabolic cost of walking under load is well established. Pandolf, Givoni & Goldman (1977), published in the Journal of Applied Physiology, developed the equation behind most load-carriage calorie estimates, including the numbers above. Later work found it reads low with heavy military loads: Looney et al. (2022), in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, reported that existing equations underestimate heavy backpacking and built a new one for the US Army. At recreational loads the picture is consistent: on flat ground a moderate pack adds a modest share to the cost of walking at the same pace, while grade adds far more.

What to do if you already walk

If you already walk 30 minutes a few times per week, you do not need a complicated training plan. Add a light load, protect your feet, and keep the pace conversational.

The upgrade is intentionally boring: keep walking, add enough weight to matter, and make the setup comfortable enough that you repeat it.

If you already walk, the missing pieces are usually a pack that holds weight high and still, the weight itself, and socks and anti-chafe for longer loaded miles. Walking shoes that already fit you can stay.

Calorie Burn Comparison

These numbers come from the Pandolf equation, the load-carriage model behind our calculator. On flat pavement each 10 lb adds roughly 15 to 20 calories an hour, rising slightly as the load gets heavier. Grade is the bigger lever: a 5 percent incline adds more than a 60-lb pack does on the flat. The equation tends to under-predict heavy loads (Drain et al., 2017, found it read 12% to 33% low with 50 to 85 lb military loads), so treat the heavier rows as conservative.

The practical point: you don't walk faster or longer to get more out of the hour. Add a hill, or add load, or both. A flat, snug-fitting plate like a cast-iron ruck weight is the simplest way to add that load without the shifting and rattle of household objects.

Why Walkers Should Ruck

More demand in the same time

The calorie estimates above show how load and grade change modeled energy expenditure. They don't measure muscle growth, posture correction or bone density.

Loaded walking also asks you to carry and stabilize a pack. That can be useful practice for hiking or an event. It doesn't replace resistance training or establish a lower injury rate than running.

What about bone health?

Research on weighted-vest exercise programs isn't proof that backpack walking alone produces the same results. Some studies combined the vest with jumping or other exercise. Our bone-density guide explains the designs and limits. Walking remains weight-bearing activity; neither option guarantees a particular change in bone density.

How Rucking Is Different From Walking

The Progression: From Walking to Rucking

Start with a short, familiar walk and a load you can carry comfortably. Keep your normal easy pace or slow down; don't add weight and hills together. Repeat that outing before deciding whether to add anything.

There isn't a universal starting weight or a week-seven sweet spot. A RUSH 12 2.0 is one cargo-pack option, but it has no dedicated plate pocket. Secure the weight rather than assuming its placement will hold itself.

Choose a load that leaves your gait controlled and the walk comfortable. Increasing slowly gives you time to assess the response; it doesn't guarantee injury prevention.

When Walking Is Actually Better

Walking is the better choice in specific situations:

Total beginners to exercise: If you have not been active in years, start with a few weeks of unloaded walking. Let your body adapt to the movement first.

Active recovery days: Ruck your main days; walk unloaded on recovery days.

Joint recovery: If you are coming back from injury, clear unloaded walking first with your PT before adding load.

Social walks: If you walk with a partner or group who is not interested in rucking, walk unloaded and maintain the social connection.

Keep ordinary walking in the week

You can walk with or without a pack according to the day's purpose. You don't have to carry weight every time, and there's no established deadline for a pack to feel normal. Reduce the load or stop when pain, numbness or a changed gait appears.

Frequently asked questions

Rucking vs walking: which is better?

Choose by the job. Rucking adds demand and practices carrying weight. Ordinary walking is simpler and may be the better fit for recovery, symptoms or an easy outing. Neither is automatically better for everyone.

How heavy should my first pack be?

Use a manageable load on a short route, or begin without one; our usual starting point is 5-10 lb for 20-30 minutes on level ground, held for the first month. A fixed 15 lb start and three-week increase schedule aren't validated for every beginner. See the weight guide.

Can I ruck every day?

Some experienced people walk with light loads frequently, but daily rucking isn't a beginner requirement. Consider total activity and recovery, and reduce frequency when symptoms or fatigue accumulate.

Is rucking better than walking for weight loss?

A pack raises estimated energy use at the same pace, but that doesn’t establish better weight loss for everyone. For a 180 lb person at 3.5 mph, the Pandolf model gives roughly 400 calories an hour with 25 lb versus 365 unloaded on flat ground. On a 5% grade, compare loaded and unloaded walking on that same incline. Food intake, the sessions you complete and changes in other activity affect the eventual result.

Will rucking hurt my knees?

Extra load changes the forces of walking; it isn't mechanically identical to an unloaded walk. Ground-reaction-force measurements don't establish which activity suits your knee best. Recurring pain calls for assessment, and if you have a diagnosed knee, heart or bone condition or are pregnant, ask your clinician before adding load; our knee-pain guide explains when to seek help.

What pack should I use?

Choose a pack suitable for the intended load, with a comfortable fit and a way to secure the weight. Purpose-built packs such as the GORUCK Rucker 5.0 provide elevated plate pockets. Hiking packs vary in fit and load guidance; check the specific model. Our gear guide explains the options.

Can I walk and ruck on the same day?

You can walk with and without a pack on the same day. There isn't a required six-hour gap. Judge the total demand, and don't treat an hour of rucking as a replacement for every kind of aerobic or strength exercise.
